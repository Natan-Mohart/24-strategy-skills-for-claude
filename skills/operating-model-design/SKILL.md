# Operating Model Design

## When to use
Use whenever a strategy has changed but the organization hasn't (a classic cause of "we have a great strategy but can't execute it"), when decisions are slow or get re-litigated because no one clearly owns them, or when someone asks for a reorg without first defining what the new structure needs to make possible.

## What it does
Designs the operating model as a coherent system — structure, decision rights, core processes, and capabilities — explicitly derived from the strategy, using McKinsey's 7S coherence check (are structure, systems, staff, skills, style, shared values, and strategy actually aligned) and RAPID/DACI to make decision rights explicit rather than assumed.

## Method
1. **Start from the strategy, not the org chart.** List the 3-5 things the organization must be able to do exceptionally well to execute the strategy (e.g., "ship product weekly," "respond to enterprise RFPs in under 48 hours") — the operating model exists to make these possible.
2. **Audit current decision rights with RAPID or DACI** (Recommend/Agree/Perform/Input/Decide, or Driver/Approver/Contributor/Informed) for the 5-10 decisions that most affect execution speed. Most "slow decision-making" problems are actually unclear decision-rights problems, not org-structure problems — fix this before redrawing boxes.
3. **Design structure to fit the required capabilities**, choosing explicitly between functional, product/business-unit, matrix, or hybrid — and state why the chosen structure best supports the capabilities named in step 1, not just "this is how peer companies are organized."
4. **Map the core processes that cut across the new structure** (how a decision actually moves from idea to shipped, how budget actually gets allocated) — a structure without process redesign just moves the same bottlenecks to new boxes.
5. **Run the 7S coherence check**: does the new structure align with systems (what gets measured/rewarded), staff (do we have the right people/roles), skills (capability gaps to close), style (how leaders actually behave), and shared values? A structurally elegant design that conflicts with incentive systems will quietly fail.
6. **Identify the capability gaps** the new model requires and aren't yet in place — hiring, reskilling, or new systems — and sequence closing them before or alongside the structural change, not after.
7. **Pilot before full rollout where possible**: one business unit or function first, with a defined evaluation window, rather than a simultaneous company-wide change.

## Inputs
- Current org structure and the strategy it needs to execute
- Known decision bottlenecks or complaints about slow execution
- Current incentive/measurement systems (to check 7S coherence)
- Any capability or skill gaps already identified

## Output format
Required capabilities derived from strategy; RAPID/DACI map for key decisions (current vs. proposed); proposed structure with rationale; core process redesign for the 2-3 most critical cross-cutting workflows; 7S coherence check; capability-gap closing plan; pilot recommendation if applicable.

## Example
A company reorganizing from functional to product-based structure to speed up shipping finds, via RAPID mapping, that the real bottleneck isn't structure at all — it's that "Approve" sits with a committee that meets monthly. Redesigning decision rights to a single accountable approver with a weekly cadence solves most of the speed problem before the structural change is even implemented, and the eventual structural change becomes smaller and lower-risk.

## Common pitfalls
- Redesigning structure without first fixing unclear decision rights, which just relocates the same bottleneck.
- Copying a peer company's org chart without checking it against this company's specific required capabilities.
- Changing structure while leaving incentive systems unchanged, so the new model is undermined by what people are still being measured and rewarded on.
